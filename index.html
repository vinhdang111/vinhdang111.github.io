French Equities Financial Analysis Live

End-to-end financial analytics covering ~50 major French-listed companies (CAC 40 / SBF 120). SQL for structured storage, Python for cleaning and revenue-growth forecasting, Power BI for an interactive dashboard.

Stack — Python, SQL, Jupyter Notebook, Power BI
Highlights — Pooled/panel regression forecasting model · sector-level profitability & leverage analysis · free cash flow margin identified as a stronger growth signal than traditional profitability ratios (ROE)

Problem

There's no single, comparable view of financial health across France's largest listed companies — profitability, leverage, and growth sit scattered across ~50 separate annual reports, making it hard to compare companies across sectors or flag which ones are actually positioned to keep growing. This project builds that comparable view from scratch on real market data, and goes one step further: forecasting which companies are likely to grow next year, not just reporting which performed well last year.

Approach

  1. Data collection — pulled ~4 years of annual income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow data for ~50 CAC 40 / SBF 120 companies via the yfinance API.
  2. SQL database — designed a normalized schema and loaded/cleaned the raw CSVs into SQLite entirely in SQL: staging tables → clean tables via CREATE TABLE + INSERT INTO ... SELECT.
  3. Ratios & EDA — computed profitability, return, leverage, liquidity, and growth ratios per company-year in Python/Jupyter, then explored cross-sector patterns.
  4. Forecasting — pooled/panel regression (Ridge vs. Random Forest) predicting next-year revenue growth from current-year ratios, with a company-grouped train/test split to prevent leakage.
  5. Dashboard — built a 3-page Power BI dashboard (Overview, Trend, Forecast) for cross-company comparison.

Key decisions & constraints

  • Used annual data only — testing showed EU-listed companies return almost no usable quarterly data via yfinance, since the EU Transparency Directive removed the mandatory quarterly-reporting requirement that still applies in the US. A market/regulatory constraint, not a data bug.
  • Switched from a per-company time series to a pooled/panel regression (one row per company-year, ~150+ rows), since ~4 years of history per company was too few observations on its own.
  • Narrowed yfinance's wide, sparse output (80–130 columns) down to 10–15 core metrics per statement in the SQL layer.
  • Excluded 2 of 50 companies from the Power BI layer (missing sector metadata / REIT structure) to keep sector comparisons consistent — documented as a scope difference from the Python analysis, which keeps the full set.
  • Applied explicit outlier handling before training: excluded a demerger-distorted YoY change (Vivendi), and winsorized extreme ratios so a near-zero-equity distress case (Atos) wouldn't dominate the regression.

Key findings

Profitability — Financial Services has the highest median net margin (~0%); Consumer Cyclical the lowest (~0%), reflecting thin retail/auto margins vs. banking economics.

Takeaway

The strongest signal wasn't the most obvious one: a company's cash-generation efficiency (FCF margin) predicted next-year growth better than traditional accounting profitability (ROE) — an insight that only surfaced once leverage effects and outliers were properly isolated.

Nestlé FP&A Case Study Live

End-to-end FP&A workflow on Nestlé's real public financials: a driver-based FY2025 budget under Adverse / Base / Favorable scenarios, built only from information available before the year, then tested against Nestlé's actual FY2025 results with a full variance analysis and driver backtest. The lessons feed a full-year FY2026 reforecast built on Nestlé's latest H1-2026 guidance, stress-tested with an interactive FX / COGS sensitivity in a 5-page Power BI dashboard.

Stack — Excel, Power BI (DAX)
Highlights — Organic growth beat the budget (3.5% vs 3.0%), but a −5.7% FX headwind against a flat assumption turned planned +3.0% sales growth into a −2.0% decline · sensitivity analysis shows how the FY2026 forecast moves with FX and COGS %
Open the workbook full screen ↗

Problem

A budget is only useful if someone checks it against what actually happened, and most budgets are never tested that way. This project plans Nestlé's FY2025 using only what was public on 13 February 2025, measures that plan against the results Nestlé published a year later, and then uses what went wrong to reforecast FY2026. The question running through it: which assumptions can a finance team control, and which ones should it stress-test instead of trying to predict?

Approach

  1. Data foundation — rebuilt Nestlé's segment and category sales and the full income statement and cash flow statement line by line for FY2021–FY2024 from the audited financial statements, with every subtotal calculated by formula.
  2. FY2025 budget — a driver-based model with ~40 assumptions covering the full P&L and cash flow, under Adverse / Base / Favorable scenarios; the ten key drivers are flexed across ranges sized from four years of history.
  3. Variance analysis & backtest — FY2025 actual vs the Base budget: a sales bridge (volume, pricing, M&A, FX), line-by-line P&L and cash flow variances, and a test of whether each flexed driver actually landed inside its scenario range.
  4. FY2026 reforecast & sensitivity — a full-year reforecast built on Nestlé's latest guidance (H1-2026 results, 23 July 2026), then one-way and two-way sensitivity on the two drivers that missed most in FY2025: FX and COGS.
  5. Dashboard — a 5-page Power BI dashboard (Overview, Budget Scenarios, Variance Analysis, Backtest, FY2026 Reforecast). On the last page, FX and COGS sliders re-run the FY2026 income statement in DAX, reconciled to the Excel model.

Key decisions & constraints

  • No hindsight — the FY2025 budget uses only information disclosed on 13 February 2025, and the scenario ranges were not touched after the actuals came out, so the variance is a real miss, not a fitted one.
  • Used Nestlé's own growth framework (organic growth = volume + pricing; reported growth adds M&A and FX), so the variance can be read in the same terms management uses. Nestlé only guided total organic growth (3.0%), so the volume / pricing split is a documented own assumption.
  • Kept the budget at Group level: Nestlé moved from 5 to 3 geographic zones on 1 January 2025, so a zone-level variance would not compare like with like.
  • Showed Nestlé's reported free cash flow next to a derived figure instead of forcing them to match, since Nestlé's definition includes adjustments it doesn't disclose separately.

Key findings

An FX story — organic growth beat the budget (0% vs 0.0%), but a −0% currency hit against a flat assumption turned planned +0.0% sales growth into a −0% decline.

Takeaway

The budget got the operating business roughly right; it missed on what management doesn't control: currency, commodity costs and one-offs. So the reforecast doesn't try to out-guess those. It takes Nestlé's own latest FX view and puts the exposure on a slider instead. For Nestlé's profit margin, COGS is the lever that matters: 10 bps of it is worth almost a full point of currency.

European Equity Volatility Forecasting Live

Benchmark of 15 volatility forecasting models, from simple rules and GARCH to LightGBM and LSTM / Transformer neural networks, on 50 large European stocks and 9 equity indices over 20 years of out-of-sample forecasts. The forecasts are then put to work in a Value-at-Risk backtest, and the results are presented in a 4-page Power BI dashboard on PostgreSQL views.

Stack — Python (pandas, LightGBM, PyTorch), PostgreSQL, SQL, Jupyter Notebook, Power BI (DAX)
Highlights — Machine learning beats the best classical model only at 1 day, by 1.3–2.4% · an equal-weight average of five models is the one reliable improvement (better in 19 of 21 years) · any volatility model cuts the share of Basel "non-green" years from 37% to 16–19% vs historical simulation

Problem

Banks, asset managers and regulators size risk with volatility forecasts every day, and machine learning is often presented as the way to make them better. But most comparisons pit a complex model against a weak benchmark, on a single market, without testing whether the gain is real. This project asks the question properly: do machine learning and deep learning beat the best classical model, is the difference statistically significant in calm and in crisis periods, and does a better forecast actually give a better risk limit?

Approach

  1. Data & storage — 26 years of daily prices for 50 stocks, 9 indices and the VIX from yfinance, stored in PostgreSQL; nine documented cleaning rules with every correction logged.
  2. Features — a range-based measure of daily variance, 18 features (own and market-wide volatility, returns, volume, VIX) and targets at 1 day, 1 week and 1 month, with unit tests against look-ahead.
  3. Models — 5 baselines, GARCH, GJR-GARCH, HAR and HAR-X, LightGBM (alone and as a correction to HAR-X), LSTM and Transformer networks in PyTorch, and two model combinations: 15 models re-estimated every year in a walk-forward backtest (2006–2026).
  4. Statistical comparison — QLIKE loss, Diebold-Mariano tests and the Model Confidence Set, results split by calm and crisis periods.
  5. Value-at-Risk — every forecast turned into a 1-day VaR and backtested on 293,000 daily returns (Kupiec and Christoffersen tests, Basel traffic light, quantile loss).
  6. Dashboard — a 4-page Power BI dashboard (Model performance, Forecast explorer, Value-at-Risk, Summary) on thirteen SQL views, with 60 DAX measures.

Key decisions & constraints

  • No look-ahead — each year is forecast by models trained only on earlier data, hyperparameters are tuned inside the training window, and an embargo stops training targets from overlapping the test year.
  • A demanding benchmark — every advanced model is compared with HAR-X, the best classical model, which uses the same information: the gap measures what the algorithm adds, not extra data.
  • Loss fixed before the results — QLIKE penalises under-estimating risk more than over-estimating it. With a symmetric error (MAE), a different model would have "won".
  • Used the VIX as the implied-volatility input, since VSTOXX, its European equivalent, has no free source.

Key findings

Small gains — at 1 day, machine learning and deep learning beat HAR-X by only 0–0%, and their average by 0% (p < 0.01). At 1 week the single models are level with it (p = 0.56–0.91), and at 1 month HAR-X is the best model, with LightGBM 0% behind.

Takeaway

Measuring volatility well and choosing the right inputs matter far more than the algorithm. For 1 to 5 days, the average of the five models; for a month, HAR-X alone.
